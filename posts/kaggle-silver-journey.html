My Journey to Kaggle Silver

May 2026

Earning my first silver medal on Kaggle was a milestone I'd been chasing for months. The competition was a tabular data challenge — the kind where feature engineering matters more than model selection, and where a single creative feature can jump you 100 places on the leaderboard.

The biggest lesson I learned: cross-validation strategy is everything. My early submissions were overfit to the public leaderboard because my CV folds weren't stratified properly. Once I switched to stratified group k-fold, my local scores finally correlated with the leaderboard, and I stopped second-guessing myself.

Let's break down the math. The cross-validation estimate of generalization error is:

$$\text{CV}_{(k)} = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \text{Err}_i = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{1}{|D_i|} \sum_{(x,y) \in D_i} \mathbb{1}[f(x) \neq y]$$

where $D_i$ is the validation set for fold $i$ and $\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function. The key insight is that if your folds aren't stratified, $\text{Err}_i$ has high variance across folds, making $\text{CV}_{(k)}$ unreliable.

Another turning point was ensemble strategy. I was stuck around the top 15% with a single LightGBM model. Stacking it with a CatBoost model and a simple neural net — each capturing different error patterns — pushed me into medal territory. The ensemble prediction is a weighted combination:

$$\hat{y} = \sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j \hat{y}_j, \quad \text{where} \quad \sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j = 1, \quad w_j \geq 0$$

The optimal weights $w_j$ minimize the validation loss $\mathcal{L}(\hat{y}, y)$, typically via constrained optimization. In practice, a simple average ($w_j = 1/m$) already captures most of the benefit.

The Kaggle community is incredibly generous with knowledge. Reading top solution writeups after every competition taught me more about practical ML than any course. Now the goal is gold — it's going to require a fundamentally different approach, and I'm ready for it.
